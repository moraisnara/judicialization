% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{1. \quad Introduction}
% ============================================================

\medskip
Electoral courts are an increasingly central institution in developing
democracies, yet their causal effects on democratic participation remain poorly
understood \citep{helmke2011,fasone2024}. Pre-election adversarial lawsuits cut
in two directions. They can improve accountability by screening unfit candidates
and deterring abuses of power \citep{assumpcao2019,ferraz2008}; but they can also
generate reputational noise that voters cannot readily evaluate, impose legal
costs on potential candidates, and be deployed strategically to harass rivals
\citep{lehoucq2003,nakaguma2025,chin2026}. The net effect is theoretically
ambiguous, and identification is difficult because competitive races attract
both more lawsuits and more candidates --- a simultaneity that defeats naive
comparisons.

Brazil is a natural laboratory. Its three-tier \textit{Justiça Eleitoral}
adjudicates eligibility and conduct cases \emph{before} election day across
\NMunis{} municipalities that vote simultaneously every four years. Adversarial
filings reached \AdvLawsuitsTwenty{}M in 2020 ---
\AdvPerCandidateTwenty{} per mayoral candidate --- and grew to
\AdvLawsuitsFortyFour{}M in 2024, making judicial involvement a pervasive
feature of municipal competition rather than an exceptional event. Brazil's
\emph{compulsory} voting (\TurnoutRateTwenty\% turnout in 2020) supplies a
distinctive channel: when abstention is penalized, voter disaffection cannot
exit as non-participation but surfaces instead as a deliberate blank ballot
\citep{power1995}. This yields a clean behavioral marker of disengagement from
the candidate slate that voluntary-voting systems do not provide.

\textit{Research question:} do pre-election adversarial challenges causally
affect voter behavior and candidate entry, and through which mechanism? Three
channels generate distinct signatures. Under \textbf{information} effects,
lawsuits alter the signal voters receive --- credibly exposing misconduct
(accountability) or merely raising opaque, strategically motivated noise
(degradation). Under \textbf{strategic targeting (lawfare)}, litigation is a
directed weapon, concentrated in the most competitive races and shifting vote
shares away from targeted rivals. Under \textbf{direct judicial action},
disqualifications mechanically remove candidates and reallocate vote shares.
The documented nature of Brazilian pre-election litigation points to
degradation: attack filings cluster among runners-up targeting front-runners,
are timed to the campaign's final days, rarely produce disqualifications, and
are difficult for voters to evaluate without legal expertise
\citep{nakaguma2025,chin2026,besley1997}. The evidence below favors this channel
over both lawfare and direct judicial action.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{2. \quad Methodology}
% ============================================================

\medskip
All data come from the TSE (\textit{Tribunal Superior Eleitoral}) for the 2020
and 2024 municipal cycles. The treatment is the change in pre-election
adversarial litigation: I retain first-instance cases filed before election day
in adversarial classes --- eligibility challenges (AIJE, AIME), registration
objections (RCAND), and conduct violations (RE, RP) --- and drop appellate and
administrative filings that carry no adversarial signal. Filings are recorded at
the electoral-zone level and aggregated to municipalities, giving the endogenous
variable $\Delta\log(1+\ell_m)=\log(1+\ell_{m,2024})-\log(1+\ell_{m,2020})$.
Outcomes span voter behavior (blank, null, turnout, and valid vote rates),
candidate entry (log candidate counts and composition in both the executive and
legislative arenas), and electoral competition (winner--runner-up margin,
runner-up vote share, winner majority). I control for Census 2010 demographics
and 2016/2020 electoral baselines, with 27 state fixed effects.

OLS is biased in two directions: competitive races attract disproportionately
more lawsuits (simultaneity), and unobserved municipal characteristics jointly
shift litigation and electoral outcomes. To recover exogenous variation I use a
\textbf{Bartik shift-share instrument} \citep{gps2020,borusyak2022}, following
\citet{ash2025}, who apply the identical structure to U.S.\ state legislative
output and growth. Each municipality's 2020 \emph{portfolio} of adversarial
lawsuits across legal topics is interacted with \emph{topic-level shocks}
originating above the municipality:

\[
  Z_m^{\textsc{b}} \;=\; \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} s_{m,k,2020}\;\cdot\;g_{\sigma(m),k,-m},
\]

where $s_{m,k,2020}$ is municipality $m$'s 2020 share of adversarial lawsuits in
topic $k$ and $g_{\sigma(m),k,-m}$ is the leave-one-out log growth of topic $k$
at the state level. Identification rests on two pillars: the 2020 portfolio is
predetermined relative to 2024 outcomes, and topic-level growth reflects
\textit{Tribunal Regional Eleitoral} jurisprudence and \textit{Ministério
Público Eleitoral} enforcement operating above the municipality, with the
leave-one-out construction ensuring no municipality drives its own shift. The
first stage is strong --- a one-unit increase in $Z_m^{\textsc{b}}$ raises
$\Delta\log(1+\ell_m)$ by \FSCoef{} (SE = \FSSE{}; $F = \FSF{}$), comfortably
above conventional weak-instrument thresholds. All specifications are estimated
by 2SLS with standard errors clustered by principal electoral zone.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{3. \quad Results}
% ============================================================

\medskip
\textbf{Voter behavior.} The headline 2SLS estimate is a \BlankCoef{}-point
increase in the blank vote rate ($p = \BlankP{}$; Table~\ref{tab:results}),
significant at the 5\% level --- a \BlankRelEffect\% increase relative to the
\BlankRateTwenty\% baseline. Its mechanical complement, the valid vote rate,
falls by \ValidCoef{} ($p = \ValidP{}$). Turnout ($p = \TurnoutP{}$) and the
null rate ($p = \NullP{}$) are precise nulls: judicialization neither
demobilizes voters nor pushes them toward spoiled ballots. The response is
confined to the blank margin --- voters who attend the polls, as compulsory
voting requires, but withhold a choice from the candidate field --- exactly the
signature predicted by information degradation. The effect is stable in sign,
magnitude, and significance across all six specifications
(Figure~\ref{fig:forest}).

\textbf{Candidate entry, competition, and gender.} Executive candidates fall by
\CandExecPct\% in log terms ($p = \CandExecP{}$) and city-council candidates by
\LegCandPct\% ($p = \LegCandP{}$), consistent with elevated legal-exposure costs
deterring entry; composition shares (new entrants, serial challengers, returners)
are indistinguishable from zero, indicating a quantity effect rather than
selection. Female vote share falls by \FemaleVSCoef{} ($p = \FemaleVSP{}$) and
the probability of a female winner by \FemaleWinCoef{} ($p = \FemaleWinP{}$);
these marginal estimates may operate through overall deterrence. The competition
margins --- winner--runner-up margin ($p = \MarginP{}$), runner-up vote share
($p = \RunnerUpP{}$), and winner majority ($p = \WinMajP{}$) --- are all precise
nulls, ruling out the direct disqualification channel, which would widen margins
as removed candidates' votes reallocate.

\textbf{Heterogeneity.} The blank-vote effect concentrates in open-seat
municipalities, where the 2020 winner was term-limited and no incumbent appears
in 2024: \OpenBlankCoef{} ($p = \OpenBlankP{}$, $N = \OpenN{}$) versus a
near-zero \ContBlankCoef{} ($p = \ContBlankP{}$) where an incumbent defends the
seat. This is the mirror image of lawfare, which predicts the largest effects in
high-stakes incumbency races; it is precisely what information degradation
implies, since open-seat voters have no incumbent track record against which to
discount an adversarial filing.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{4. \quad Conclusion}
% ============================================================

\medskip
This paper provides the first credibly identified estimates of how pre-election
electoral-court activity affects democratic participation and candidate entry.
Exploiting a shift-share instrument built from Brazil's administrative processual
records, I find that adversarial judicialization raises blank voting, deters
candidate entry across both executive and legislative arenas, and leaves vote
margins and the composition of the candidate pool unchanged. The pattern ---
disengagement directed at the candidate field rather than at the act of voting,
nulls on competition margins, and effects concentrated where voters lack an
incumbent benchmark --- is consistent with information degradation and
inconsistent with both strategic lawfare and direct judicial removal.

Two lessons generalize beyond Brazil. First, where voting is compulsory,
political disengagement is channeled into blank ballots rather than abstention,
making the blank margin an observable equilibrium response to information
degradation that voluntary-voting systems obscure. Second, that the same
candidate-deterrence effect appears in plurality (mayoral) and open-list
proportional (council) elections points to fixed legal and reputational costs of
candidacy under elevated litigation. The policy implication is direct: expanding
judicial enforcement in the electoral domain --- a standard governance reform ---
carries measurable participation costs, and the trade-off between legal integrity
and democratic dynamism deserves explicit attention, particularly under
compulsory voting. The design identifies the effect of differential growth in
adversarial filings rather than the level of judicialization, and the estimates
should be read accordingly.
