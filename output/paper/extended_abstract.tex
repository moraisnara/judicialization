% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{1. \quad Introduction}
% ============================================================

\medskip
In Latin American democracies, the judiciary increasingly shapes electoral
competition, with growing authority over who may run and how campaigns are
conducted \citep{helmke2011,fasone2024,marchetti2012}.
Electoral litigation cuts in two directions. It can protect democracy, screening
unfit candidates and deterring abuses of power \citep{assumpcao2019,ferraz2008},
yet it can also operate as a political weapon, surrounding candidates with
reputational noise that voters cannot readily evaluate and imposing legal costs on
candidates \citep{lehoucq2003,nakaguma2025,chin2026}. The net effect is
theoretically ambiguous, and recovering it is hard because litigation and
competition are jointly determined: more lawsuits may reshape a race, but more
competitive races also attract more lawsuits.

Brazil is a natural laboratory. Its three-tier \textit{Justiça Eleitoral} not only organizes the elections but also adjudicates
eligibility and conduct cases across \NMunis{} municipalities that vote simultaneously every four years. Adversarial
filings reached \AdvLawsuitsTwenty{}M in 2020 and grew to
\AdvLawsuitsFortyFour{}M in 2024, making judicial involvement a pervasive feature of municipal competition rather than an exceptional event. In 2020, 30\% of mayoral candidates were involved in an electoral lawsuit \citep{chin2026}, an
average of \AdvPerCandidateTwenty{} per candidate in our sample of first-instance cases.

Given that, one might ask: do election lawsuits causally affect voter behavior
and candidate performance, and through which mechanism? We argue that three
channels generate distinct signatures. Under \textbf{information} effects,
lawsuits alter the signal voters receive, either credibly exposing misconduct
(accountability) or merely raising opaque, strategically motivated noise
(degradation). Under \textbf{strategic targeting (lawfare)}, litigation is a
directed weapon, concentrated in the most competitive races and shifting vote
shares away from targeted rivals. Under \textbf{direct judicial action},
disqualifications mechanically remove candidates and reallocate vote shares.
The documented nature of Brazilian pre-election litigation points to
degradation: attack filings cluster among runners-up targeting front-runners,
are timed to the campaign's final days, rarely produce disqualifications, and
are difficult for voters to evaluate without legal expertise
\citep{nakaguma2025,chin2026,besley1997}. The evidence below favors this channel
over both lawfare and direct judicial action.

This paper measures how judicial intervention in elections affects democratic
participation and candidate performance. It overcomes the central identification
problem by using a shift-share instrument built from the flow of adversarial filings, following \cite{ash2025},
isolating variation in judicialization driven by enforcement above the
municipality rather than by local competitiveness.

Our findings speak to two literatures. Whereas work on courts and elections in
Latin America has characterized the judiciary's electoral role in largely
qualitative and institutional terms \citep{helmke2011,fasone2024,marchetti2012},
we provide the first causal estimates of its effect on voter behavior and
candidate entry. And whereas recent studies of electoral litigation in Brazil
ask how a lawsuit shapes the targeted candidate's fortunes
\citep{chin2026,nakaguma2025}, we shift the unit of analysis to the
local election, estimating how judicialization moves aggregate voter
behavior and the composition of the candidate pool.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{2. \quad Methodology}
% ============================================================

\medskip
All data come from the TSE (\textit{Tribunal Superior Eleitoral}) for the 2020
and 2024 municipal cycles. The treatment is the change in pre-election adversarial litigation: we retain first-instance cases filed before election day
in adversarial classes --- eligibility challenges (AIJE, AIME), registration
objections (RCAND), and conduct violations (RE, RP) --- and drop appellate and
administrative filings that carry no adversarial signal. Filings are recorded at
the electoral-zone level and aggregated to the municipality. The treatment is the log
change $\Delta\log(1+\ell_m)=\log(1+\ell_{m,2024})-\log(1+\ell_{m,2020})$, where
$\ell_{m,t}$ counts adversarial filings in municipality $m$ in year $t$ and the
$\log(1+\cdot)$ form accommodates municipalities with no filings.
Outcomes span voter behavior (blank, null, turnout, and valid vote rates),
candidate entry (log candidate counts and composition in both the executive and
legislative arenas), and electoral competition (winner--runner-up margin,
runner-up vote share, winner majority). We control for Census 2010 demographics
and 2016/2020 electoral baselines, with 27 state fixed effects.

Competitive races attract disproportionately
more lawsuits, and unobserved municipal characteristics jointly
shift litigation and electoral outcomes, which complicates identifying the effect of judicialization on electoral competition. To recover exogenous variation, we use a
\textbf{Bartik shift-share instrument} \citep{gps2020,borusyak2022}, following
\citet{ash2025}, who apply the identical structure to U.S.\ state legislative
output and growth. Each municipality's 2020 \emph{portfolio} of adversarial
lawsuits across legal topics is interacted with \emph{topic-level shocks}
originating above the municipality:

\[
  Z_m^{\textsc{b}} \;=\; \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} s_{m,k,2020}\;\cdot\;g_{\sigma(m),k,-m},
\]

where $s_{m,k,2020}$ is municipality $m$'s 2020 share of adversarial lawsuits in
topic $k$ and $g_{\sigma(m),k,-m}$ is the leave-one-out log growth of topic $k$
at the state level. Identification rests on two pillars: the 2020 portfolio is
predetermined relative to 2024 outcomes, and topic-level growth reflects the Electoral Justice enforcement operating above the municipality, with the
leave-one-out construction ensuring no municipality drives its own shift. The
first stage is strong: a one-unit increase in $Z_m^{\textsc{b}}$ raises
$\Delta\log(1+\ell_m)$ by \FSCoef{} (SE = \FSSE{}; $F = \FSF{}$), comfortably
above conventional weak-instrument thresholds. All specifications are estimated
by 2SLS with standard errors clustered by principal electoral zone.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{3. \quad Results}
% ============================================================

\medskip
Each outcome group maps to one channel. Voter behavior (Panel~A) tests
\textbf{information}: degradation predicts blank votes rising while turnout and
spoiled ballots stay flat. Candidate performance (Panel~B) separates entry
deterrence from \textbf{lawfare} and \textbf{direct judicial action}, which would
move competition margins; the open-seat split (Panel~C) is the decisive test.

\medskip
\textbf{Voter behavior.} The primary result is a positive
\BlankCoefPP{}-percentage-point increase in the blank-vote rate, significant at
the 5\% level ($p = \BlankP{}$; Table~\ref{tab:results}) --- a \BlankRelEffect\%
rise relative to the \BlankRateTwenty\% baseline. Its mechanical complement, the valid vote rate,
falls by \ValidCoefPP{} percentage points ($p = \ValidP{}$). Turnout ($p = \TurnoutP{}$) and the
null rate ($p = \NullP{}$) are precise nulls: judicialization neither
demobilizes voters nor pushes them toward spoiled ballots.%
\footnote{Because the turnout effect is a precise null, the blank-vote
response reflects \emph{existing} voters withholding a choice rather than a
change in the composition of who turns out: the same electorate shows up, but
more of it declines to choose among the candidates. A compositional account
would require judicialization to move turnout, which it does not.} The response is
confined to the blank margin --- voters who attend the polls, as compulsory
voting requires, but withhold a choice from the candidate field --- exactly the
signature predicted by information degradation. The effect is stable in sign,
magnitude, and significance across all six specifications
(Figure~\ref{fig:forest}).

\textbf{Candidate performance.} Executive candidates fall by roughly
\CandExecPct\% ($p = \CandExecP{}$) and city-council candidates by about
\LegCandPct\% ($p = \LegCandP{}$), consistent with elevated legal-exposure costs
deterring entry; entrant-type shares (new entrants, serial challengers, returners)
are indistinguishable from zero, indicating a quantity effect rather than
selection. The competition margins --- winner--runner-up margin
($p = \MarginP{}$), runner-up vote share ($p = \RunnerUpP{}$), and winner majority
($p = \WinMajP{}$) --- are all precise nulls, ruling out the direct
disqualification channel, which would widen margins as removed candidates' votes
reallocate.

\smallskip
The sharpest cost falls on women. The female share of executive candidates
\emph{rose} nationally across the two cycles (from \FemaleShareExecTwenty\% in
2020 to \FemaleShareExecFortyFour\% in 2024), yet judicialization pushes the
opposite way: female vote share falls by \FemaleVSCoefPP{} percentage points
($p = \FemaleVSP{}$) and the probability of a female winner by \FemaleWinCoefPP{}
percentage points ($p = \FemaleWinP{}$), while the female share of the candidate
pool itself drops by \FemaleCandCoefPP{} percentage points ($p = \FemaleCandP{}$).
Because women were gaining ground on average, the estimated suppression is, if
anything, more striking --- the effect is a genuine reduction in support for and
representation of women, working against a rising tide rather than riding it.

\textbf{Heterogeneity.} The blank-vote effect concentrates in open-seat
municipalities, where the 2020 winner was term-limited:
a \OpenBlankCoefPP{}-percentage-point rise ($p = \OpenBlankP{}$,
$N = \OpenN{}$) versus a near-zero \ContBlankCoefPP{} percentage points
($p = \ContBlankP{}$) where an incumbent defends the seat. This is the mirror image of lawfare, which predicts the largest effects in
high-stakes incumbency races; it is precisely what information degradation
implies, since open-seat voters have no incumbent track record against which to
discount an adversarial filing.

% ============================================================
\noindent\textbf{4. \quad Conclusion}
% ============================================================

\medskip
This paper provides the first credibly identified estimates of how pre-election
electoral-court activity affects democratic participation and candidate entry.
Exploiting a shift-share instrument built from Brazil's administrative processual
records, we find that adversarial judicialization raises blank voting, deters
candidate entry across both executive and legislative arenas, and leaves vote
margins and the composition of the candidate pool unchanged. The pattern---disengagement
directed at the candidate field rather than at the act of voting,
nulls on competition margins, and effects concentrated where voters lack an
incumbent benchmark---is consistent with information degradation and
inconsistent with both strategic lawfare and direct judicial removal.

Two lessons generalize beyond Brazil. First, where voting is compulsory, political disengagement is channeled into blank ballots rather than abstention,
making the blank margin an observable equilibrium response to information degradation that voluntary-voting systems obscure. Second, that the same
candidate-deterrence effect appears in plurality (mayoral) and open-list proportional (council) elections points to fixed legal and reputational costs of
candidacy under elevated litigation. The policy implication is direct: expanding
judicial enforcement in the electoral domain 
carries measurable participation costs, and the trade-off between legal integrity
and democratic dynamism deserves explicit attention, particularly under
compulsory voting. The design identifies the effect of differential growth in
adversarial filings rather than the level of judicialization, and the estimates
should be read accordingly.
